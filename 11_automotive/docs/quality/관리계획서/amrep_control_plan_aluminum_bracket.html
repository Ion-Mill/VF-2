Control Plan in Manufacturing: Types, Requirements, and Practical Example (2026)

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Control Plan in Manufacturing: Types, Requirements, and Practical Example (2026)

By AMREP | Posted on September 7, 2026

A control plan is a structured document that specifies how each manufacturing process step is monitored and controlled to ensure consistent product quality. It defines what is measured, the specification, the measurement method, the frequency, and the action to take when the process goes out of control. There are three types, Prototype, Pre-Launch, and Production, each corresponding to a phase in the product lifecycle. Under IATF 16949 Clause 8.5.1.1, a production control plan is mandatory and must always reflect the current process. Section 8.5.1.1 consistently ranks in the top four sources of IATF audit findings, according to the International Automotive Oversight Bureau (IAOB).

This guide covers what a control plan is, the three types, every column explained, IATF 16949 and AIAG requirements, and a practical worked example.

What Is a Control Plan?

A control plan is a structured document that describes the systems and processes required to control product quality during manufacturing. Under IATF 16949:2016, it is the documented description of the systems and processes required for controlling the manufacturing of products. It is not a work instruction or inspection checklist, though it connects directly to both. It is the master reference that governs in-process control for every production run.

A well-built control plan answers four questions at every process step:

  • What characteristic are we controlling?
  • How are we measuring it?
  • How often are we checking it?
  • What do we do when it falls out of specification?

Why Control Plans Matter

Regulatory and customer requirement. Control plans are a mandatory PPAP deliverable under the AIAG Production Part Approval Process, required by IATF 16949 Clause 8.5.1.1, and specified in most OEM customer-specific requirements. A missing or outdated control plan is a PPAP rejection reason and an IATF audit non-conformance.

Operational continuity. When operators change or machines are replaced, the control plan ensures the process runs the same way every time. It is the institutional memory of how a process is supposed to work.

Cost of non-detection. A process without effective controls produces defects that travel downstream. The cost of a defect caught on the production floor is a fraction of the cost caught at the customer.

Audit performance. Most IATF 16949 Section 8.5.1.1 findings trace to one gap: the control plan does not match the current production process or current PFMEA revision.

The Three Types of Control Plans

Control plan development is a key APQP output, with three types corresponding to product lifecycle phases. All three follow the same column structure, only scope, timing, and detail level change.

Prototype Control Plan

Covers early builds during product design and development. Its purpose is design validation before committing to production tooling. Controls are broader and less prescriptive since the process is not yet defined. A prototype control plan is required only if the customer mandates it, confirm with your customer's APQP requirements.

Pre-Launch Control Plan

Governs pilot runs and the PPAP build event. Controls may be stricter because the process is still being stabilized. This is the version submitted in the PPAP package and may include additional supervision methods such as the GP-12 procedure. The 2024 AIAG Control Plan manual formalized a Safe Launch phase as a distinct element within this stage.

Production Control Plan

The live document governing full-volume serial manufacturing. Contains a comprehensive listing of product and process special characteristics, controls, and measurement methods. This is what auditors review during IATF 16949 Clause 8.5.1.1 audits and must always reflect the current process.

IATF 16949 and AIAG Requirements (2024 Update)

IATF 16949 Clause 8.5.1.1

The control plan must:

  • List controls used for manufacturing process control
  • Include monitoring methods for special characteristics (customer and organization-defined)
  • Initiate the specified reaction plan when the process becomes unstable or not statistically capable
  • Be reviewed and updated when any change occurs affecting product quality

AIAG Control Plan Manual, 1st Edition (March 2024)

AIAG released a standalone Control Plan Reference Manual effective March 1, 2024. GM and Stellantis required implementation by September 1, 2024; Ford by December 31, 2024. Key changes include alignment of terminology with IATF 16949:2016 and the AIAG/VDA FMEA Handbook, formalization of the Safe Launch phase, and strengthened PFMEA linkage requirements.

Customer-Specific Requirements

Most OEMs add to control plan obligations through CSRs, including customer approval before shipment for changes to submitted characteristics, specific safe launch sample sizes, required special characteristic symbols, and portal submission requirements (e.g., GM's SCMS system).

Process Step Columns

  • Part / Process Number: Sequential step number matching the Process Flow Diagram.
  • Process Name / Operation Description: Operation name identical to the Process Flow Diagram (e.g., “CNC Turning,” “Heat Treatment”).
  • Machine, Device, Jig, Tools: Specific equipment or fixture ID. Generic entries like “CNC machine” are not acceptable.

Characteristic Columns

  • Number: Sequential characteristic reference number. Must align with the PFMEA.
  • Product Characteristic: The product feature being controlled (diameter, surface finish, hardness). Derived from the drawing.
  • Process Characteristic: The process parameter being controlled (temperature, pressure, feed rate). These are controlled inputs that drive product outputs.
  • Special Characteristic Classification: The symbol designating a characteristic as special.

Specification and Method Columns

  • Specification / Tolerance: Traceable to a specific drawing or document revision.
  • Evaluation / Measurement Technique: Specific instrument with type and resolution (not just “caliper”). Must correspond to a gauge that has completed MSA.
  • Sample Size: Statistically justified for SPC applications. Customer CSRs may mandate minimum sizes for special characteristics.
  • Sample Frequency: Based on process risk and capability data. If capability data is not yet established, run a Process Capability Study before setting frequencies for special characteristics.
  • Control Method: SPC, go/no-go, poka-yoke, visual inspection, etc. Must match the detection method in the corresponding PFMEA row.
  • Reaction Plan: Specific action when the process goes out of control. Must specify containment scope, segregation, and re-verification steps, not just “notify supervisor.”

Special Characteristics: Understanding the Symbols

Special characteristics are product or process parameters where variation beyond specification limits could compromise safety, regulatory compliance, or fit/function. They require elevated controls: mandatory SPC, more frequent measurement, and customer notification for any change.

The same special characteristic must appear in both the PFMEA and the control plan with the same symbol. Misalignment between the two documents is one of the most common PPAP rejection causes.

The PFMEA and Control Plan Link

The control plan is a direct output of the Process FMEA (PFMEA). If you are new to FMEA, read our FMEA Quick Guide before building your control plan.

The PFMEA identifies potential failure modes and defines detection controls at each process step. The control plan specifies how those detection controls are implemented, the specific instrument, sample size, frequency, and reaction plan. The detection control in the PFMEA must match the control method in the control plan for every characteristic.

One of the top PPAP rejection reasons in automotive supplier quality is “Control Plan does not reflect current PFMEA detection controls,” caused when the control plan is built from an older PFMEA revision.

The Three-Document Triad

  • Process Flow Diagram (PFD) defines the sequence of process steps
  • PFMEA identifies risks and defines detection controls at each step
  • Control Plan specifies how those controls are implemented in production

Operation numbers, process step names, and characteristic numbers must be consistent across all three. An auditor will lay all three side by side and trace each entry.

When to Review and Update Your Control Plan

The control plan must be reviewed and updated when any change occurs affecting the product, manufacturing process, measurement system, supply sources, or risk analysis (PFMEA). Specific triggers include:

  • Engineering changes (drawing revisions, tolerance changes)
  • Process changes (new tooling, new machine, revised parameters)
  • Measurement system changes (new gauge, revised calibration frequency)
  • Supplier changes (raw material or sub-tier supplier)
  • Customer complaints related to a controlled characteristic
  • PFMEA revision affecting detection controls
  • Internal or external audit findings requiring corrective action

If required by the customer, approval must be obtained after review or revision. For characteristics on a submitted PPAP, changes without customer notification are a major non-conformance under most OEM CSRs.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

  • Control plan does not match the PFMEA. The leading cause of PPAP rejections and IATF findings. Cross-reference both documents every time either is revised.
  • Reaction plans are too vague. “Notify supervisor” is not a reaction plan. Specify containment scope, segregation, and re-verification steps. When a reaction plan is triggered, the outcome typically requires a formal Non-Conformance Report (NCR) to document the deviation and drive corrective action.
  • Sample sizes are not justified. Entering “5 parts per shift” without statistical justification is inadequate for special characteristics. Base sample sizes on process capability and detection sensitivity required.
  • Process characteristics missing. A control plan that monitors only product outputs cannot prevent defects, it can only detect them after the fact. Include process inputs (temperature, pressure, feed rates).
  • Outdated control plans in production. The most common finding in AMREP's supplier audits. Implement document control that ensures the production floor always has the current revision.
  • Missing or incorrect special characteristic symbols. A symbol present in the PFMEA must carry through to the control plan. Dropping it, even unintentionally, is a PPAP rejection reason.
  • Generic equipment entries. “Caliper” or “CMM” are not sufficient. Specify the instrument, resolution, and calibration status.

Control Plan Development Checklist

Phase 1: Preparation

  • Process Flow Diagram completed and approved
  • PFMEA completed, reviewed, and linked to this control plan revision
  • Customer-specific requirements reviewed for control plan obligations
  • Special characteristic symbols confirmed per customer requirements
  • MSA completed for all measurement methods listed

Phase 2: Header Completion

  • Control plan type identified (Prototype / Pre-Launch / Production)
  • Part number and latest drawing revision confirmed
  • Key contact assigned; all approval signatures obtained

Phase 3: Process Step Entries

  • Operation numbers and names match Process Flow Diagram exactly
  • Specific machine/tool/fixture identified for each operation
  • Both product and process characteristics listed per operation
  • All special characteristics from PFMEA carried over with correct symbols

Phase 4: Specification and Method Entries

  • All specifications traceable to current drawing or document revision
  • Measurement method specifies instrument type and resolution
  • Sample sizes statistically justified or per customer CSR
  • Control methods match PFMEA detection controls

Phase 5: Reaction Plans

  • Every characteristic has a specific reaction plan
  • Reaction plans specify containment scope and re-verification step before restart
  • Operators trained on reaction plan execution

Phase 6: Review and Approval

  • Cross-referenced against current PFMEA, all detection controls aligned
  • Cross-referenced against current Process Flow Diagram, all operations aligned
  • Customer approval obtained if required by CSR
  • Production floor copy updated to current revision

Frequently Asked Questions

Who is responsible for creating and maintaining the control plan?

The supplier quality engineer or quality manager typically owns it, but it should be developed by a cross-functional team including manufacturing, engineering, and quality. IATF 16949 does not assign a single role. What matters is that the responsible person has authority to update it when changes occur and that operators are trained on its contents.

Can one control plan cover multiple part numbers?

Yes. A family control plan is acceptable when parts share the same process steps, machines, and controls, differing only in dimensions or materials. Each part number must be listed in the header, and tolerances that differ between parts must be called out separately. Auditors will verify that family plans reflect genuine process similarity, not administrative convenience.

How detailed does a reaction plan need to be for a non-special characteristic?

Specific enough for an operator to act without escalating. At minimum, it should state what to do immediately (flag, segregate, hold parts), how many parts to contain (back to the last known good sample), and what must happen before production resumes. “Rework” or “scrap” alone is not a reaction plan.

What is the difference between a control plan and a control chart?

A control plan defines what to monitor, how, how often, and what to do when results fall out of limits. A control chart (SPC chart) is one control method listed within the plan, used to track a specific characteristic over time. The control plan tells you to use it; the control chart is where you use it.

Does a control plan need to be re-submitted to the customer after every update?

Not always. Changes affecting a characteristic on a submitted PPAP almost always require customer approval before implementation. Minor administrative updates, such as correcting a contact name or updating a machine number with no process impact, typically do not. Confirm the threshold with your customer's CSR.

What happens to the control plan during a safe launch period?

Controls are tighter than standard production: higher sample sizes, more frequent measurement, and sometimes 100% inspection on critical characteristics. Once the process demonstrates stability over the customer-defined period, the control plan is updated to reflect reduced sampling frequencies. Safe launch exit criteria should be documented in the control plan itself.

Can a control plan be used as a training document for operators?

As a reference, yes. But it should not replace a work instruction. The control plan specifies what to measure and what to do when something is wrong. It does not contain step-by-step instructions for operating. Both documents should be accessible at the workstation.

What is the difference between a process control plan and a product control plan?

These are not separate document types under IATF 16949. A single control plan covers both product characteristics (dimensions, attributes) and process characteristics (temperatures, pressures, speeds). Product characteristics come from the drawing; process characteristics come from the PFMEA. Both must be present for the plan to be effective.
